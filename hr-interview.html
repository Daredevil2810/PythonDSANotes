HR & behavioural interview

Practise concise, honest answers about your experience, projects, teamwork, learning, strengths and career goals.

Tell me about yourself.

Give a short professional story: where you are now, the skills you have built, the projects or experience that demonstrate them, and what kind of role you are looking for next. Keep the answer focused on the job rather than giving a complete life history.

Tip: Aim for about 60–90 seconds and finish by connecting your experience to the role.

Why do you want to join our company?

Mention something specific about the company's product, engineering work, mission, customers or growth, then connect it to what you want to learn and contribute. Avoid an answer that could be pasted unchanged for any company.

Tip: Research the company before the interview and name one or two concrete reasons.

Why do you want this role?

Explain why the responsibilities match your current skills and why the role gives you room to grow. Connect your Python, DSA, project or web-development preparation to the actual work described in the job posting.

Tip: Show both what you can contribute now and what you want to develop.

What are your strengths?

Choose two or three strengths that matter for the role, such as structured problem solving, learning quickly, communication or persistence. Give a short example that proves each strength instead of only listing adjectives.

Tip: Evidence makes a strength credible.

What is your biggest weakness?

Choose a genuine but manageable weakness that does not undermine a core requirement of the role. Explain what you noticed, what you are doing to improve it, and what has changed as a result.

Tip: Do not disguise a strength as a weakness or say that you have no weaknesses.

Tell me about a project you are proud of.

Use a simple structure: problem, your responsibility, approach, important technical decisions, result and what you learned. Be ready to explain the architecture and code at a deeper level if the interviewer asks follow-up questions.

Tip: Know every project listed on your resume well enough to defend your decisions.

What was the biggest challenge in your project?

Describe a real technical or execution challenge, explain why it was difficult, what options you considered, what you chose, and the result. End with the lesson you would apply next time.

Tip: The interviewer is evaluating your reasoning and ownership, not whether the project was perfect.

What exactly was your contribution to the project?

Separate your own work from team or tutorial work. Explain the features, code, debugging, design decisions or testing you personally handled and the outcome of that work.

Tip: Be specific; avoid saying only 'we built' when asked about your contribution.

Tell me about a time you worked in a team.

Explain the team goal, your responsibility, how you coordinated with others, a challenge or disagreement if relevant, and the final result. Highlight communication and reliability as well as technical contribution.

Tip: Use a real example rather than describing teamwork in theory.

Tell me about a disagreement with a teammate.

Describe the disagreement objectively, explain how you listened to the other view, compared the options using evidence or project requirements, and reached a practical decision. Avoid blaming the other person.

Tip: Show that disagreement can be handled professionally without becoming personal.

How do you handle constructive criticism?

Listen first, clarify what needs improvement, decide which action is useful, and then apply the feedback. If the feedback is unclear, ask for a concrete example or expected outcome.

Tip: A strong answer shows that feedback changes your behavior, not just that you politely accept it.

How do you give feedback to a teammate?

Focus on observable behavior and its impact, discuss it privately and respectfully, and suggest a practical improvement. Invite the teammate's perspective instead of assuming intent.

Tip: Talk about the work or behavior, not the person's character.

Tell me about a time you had a tight deadline.

Explain how you broke the work into priorities, identified what was essential, communicated risks early, and delivered the most important outcome. Mention any trade-off you made and why.

Tip: Good prioritization is often more valuable than claiming you simply worked longer.

Tell me about a failure or mistake.

Choose a real example where you had responsibility. Explain what went wrong, how you responded, what you changed, and how you prevent a repeat. Avoid choosing a fake failure with no consequence.

Tip: Spend more time on the lesson and changed behavior than on defending yourself.

How do you handle pressure?

Break pressure into concrete tasks, identify the highest-priority work, communicate constraints, and work systematically rather than reacting to every request at once. Mention a real example if possible.

Tip: Do not imply that pressure never affects you; show how you manage it.

How do you prioritize when everything seems important?

Clarify deadlines, business or user impact, dependencies and risk. Handle urgent high-impact blockers first, then schedule the remaining work. If priorities conflict, confirm them with the relevant stakeholder.

Tip: Explain how you make the priority decision instead of saying you just multitask.

How do you learn a new technology?

Start with the official fundamentals, build a small working example, then use the technology in a realistic project. When blocked, read documentation, inspect examples and test assumptions rather than copying code blindly.

Tip: Connect the answer to something you have actually learned, such as Python, DSA or Django.

What do you do when you do not know the answer?

First clarify the question and identify what I do know. Then break the problem into smaller parts, state assumptions, reason from known concepts, and use reliable documentation or experiments when appropriate. I would communicate uncertainty rather than invent an answer.

Tip: Interviewers often care more about your reasoning process than instant recall.

How do you approach a difficult bug?

Reproduce the issue, reduce it to the smallest useful case, inspect the inputs and assumptions, form a hypothesis, test it, and then verify the fix with a regression check. I would also document the root cause when it could recur.

Tip: A structured debugging process is stronger than saying you start changing random lines until it works.

How do you explain a technical concept to a non-technical person?

Start with the outcome or problem, remove unnecessary jargon, use a simple example only when it clarifies the idea, and check whether the listener needs more detail. I would adjust the explanation to the person's role and goal.

Tip: Good communication is about matching the explanation to the listener, not showing how much terminology you know.

How do you stay productive when working independently?

Keep clear priorities, break work into small deliverables, track progress, and communicate blockers early. I also create regular checkpoints so independent work does not become silent work.

Tip: Mention tools or habits only if you actually use them.

What do you expect from your manager?

I value clear priorities, useful feedback, context behind important decisions and enough ownership to solve problems independently. I also expect direct communication when my work needs to improve.

Tip: Avoid making the manager responsible for your motivation or every decision.

What kind of work environment helps you perform well?

I work best in an environment with clear goals, respectful communication, reasonable ownership and a willingness to discuss technical decisions openly. I can adapt to different team structures when expectations are clear.

Tip: Keep the answer compatible with the actual role rather than describing an unrealistic perfect workplace.

Where do you see yourself in three to five years?

I want to become a dependable software engineer who can own meaningful features, make sound technical decisions and help others. I expect the exact technology to evolve, so I focus on building strong engineering fundamentals while growing into greater responsibility.

Tip: Show direction and ambition without pretending you know your exact future title.

What are your short-term career goals?

My immediate goal is to become productive in a real engineering team, strengthen my coding and problem-solving skills, understand production practices, and take ownership of increasingly useful work.

Tip: Tie the goal to the role you are applying for.

How would you explain a gap or change in your experience?

Explain the period honestly and briefly, then focus on what you learned, built or changed during it and why you are now ready for the role. Keep the explanation professional and avoid unnecessary personal detail.

Tip: The goal is clarity and forward direction, not a long defense.

Why should we hire you?

Combine the strongest evidence from your background: relevant technical skills, projects or problem-solving ability, willingness to learn, and the specific value you can bring to this role. Support the claims with examples.

Tip: Do not compare yourself aggressively with other candidates; explain your own fit.

What are your salary expectations?

If the company has a published range, use it as the starting point. Otherwise, give a researched range based on the role, location, experience and market, while showing that you are considering the overall opportunity as well.

Tip: Do your market research before the interview and avoid inventing a number on the spot.

Do you have any questions for us?

Yes. Ask questions that help you understand the role, team, expectations, engineering practices and success criteria. Examples include: What would success look like in the first six months? What kind of problems would I work on? How does the team review and ship code?

Tip: Prepare several questions and choose the ones that were not already answered during the interview.

When can you start?

Give your actual availability clearly, including any notice period or commitments you need to complete. If you have flexibility, say what range is realistic rather than promising an impossible date.

Tip: Be accurate; a clear commitment is better than an optimistic date you cannot meet.
