Pie charts: parts of a whole

Draw a pie chart, print percentages on the slices, pull a slice out and keep it readable.

1What is it?

A pie chart shows how a total is split into parts. Each slice is one part, and its angle is proportional to its share of the whole.

2Why do we need it?

When the question is what share of the total is each part?, a pie answers it quickly: the biggest slice is the biggest part. It is common in budgets, survey results and market-share summaries.

3In plain words

Everyday example

Think of a pizza cut into slices of different sizes. You immediately see who got the biggest slice. A pie chart is that pizza, drawn from your numbers.

Precise definition

plt.pie(values, labels=names) draws the chart. autopct='%1.1f%%' prints each slice's percentage (the format means one decimal place, then a percent sign). explode=[...] pulls slices out, startangle rotates the start, and plt.axis('equal') keeps the pie round.

  1. Prepare the values and their names.
  2. Call plt.pie(values, labels=names).
  3. Add autopct to show percentages.
  4. Use explode to emphasise one slice.
  5. Keep the number of slices small.

4Code, step by step

A basic pie chart

lesson_1.py
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

spend = [12000, 6000, 3000, 9000]
names = ['Rent', 'Food', 'Travel', 'Savings']

plt.pie(spend, labels=names)
plt.show()
LineWhat it means
spend = [...]The amounts. Matplotlib works out each share from the total, so you do not need to convert to percentages.
labels=namesWrites a name beside each slice, in the same order as the values.

Percentages, colours and a slice pulled out

lesson_2.py
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

spend = [12000, 6000, 3000, 9000]
names = ['Rent', 'Food', 'Travel', 'Savings']
colours = ['#2563eb', '#f59e0b', '#ef4444', '#0d9488']

plt.pie(spend, labels=names, colors=colours,
        autopct='%1.1f%%', startangle=90,
        explode=[0, 0, 0, 0.1])
plt.title('Where my money goes')
plt.show()
LineWhat it means
colors=coloursOne colour per slice. This argument is spelled colors in pie.
autopct='%1.1f%%'Prints each slice's share with one decimal place. The doubled %% produces a single % sign.
startangle=90Starts the first slice at the top instead of the right.
explode=[0, 0, 0, 0.1]Moves slices away from the centre. Here only the last slice, Savings, is pulled out.

A legend instead of labels

lesson_3.py
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

spend = [12000, 6000, 3000, 9000]
names = ['Rent', 'Food', 'Travel', 'Savings']

plt.pie(spend, autopct='%1.0f%%')
plt.legend(names, loc='center left', bbox_to_anchor=(1, 0.5))
plt.axis('equal')
plt.show()
LineWhat it means
plt.pie(spend, autopct='%1.0f%%')No labels here, so the slices stay uncluttered. 1.0f shows whole percentages.
plt.legend(names, loc=..., bbox_to_anchor=(1, 0.5))Puts a legend to the right of the pie instead of writing names on it.
plt.axis('equal')Makes the x and y scales equal, so the pie stays a circle.

5Common mistakes

  • Using a pie with many slices. Past five or six, a bar chart is much easier to read.
  • Using a pie for values that are not parts of one whole, such as temperatures in different cities.
  • Passing negative values. Pie slices must be zero or more.
  • Giving labels or explode lists of a different length than the values.

6When should I use it?

Use a pie chart only when you want to show shares of a single total with a handful of parts. If you need to compare sizes precisely, use a bar chart.

7Interview questions

How do you show percentages on a pie chart?

Use autopct, for example autopct='%1.1f%%'.

What does explode do?

It pulls slices away from the centre by a fraction of the radius, to emphasise them.

When is a pie chart a poor choice?

When there are many categories or when the parts do not add up to a meaningful whole. A bar chart is clearer.

Why use plt.axis('equal')?

It keeps the chart perfectly round instead of stretched into an oval.

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
